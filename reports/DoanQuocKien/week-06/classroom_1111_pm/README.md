## Result: autonomous arrival at a legal sweet spot (`approach_door`, run `ap_1`)
The robot started with the door behind-right (about 3.42 m by tape, about -130 deg), spun right to find it, then repeated: measure the door (median of 5 frames: bearing from the entrance detector, outline area) -> turn a short pulse until the bearing is within +/- 4 deg, or drive a 0.6 s burst at v = 0.85 -> stop, hold 0.8 s, measure again. Range = 3.42 m * sqrt(A0 / A) with A0 the outline area of the first sighting (the only manual number is that tape value). It stopped at an estimated 1.97 m after 51 steps, 10 drive bursts, about 2.5 minutes, no human input during the run.
**Tape afterwards: 1.67 m, pointing at the door centre.** The legal window is 1.5-3.0 m, so this is a usable sweet spot. Range-from-area error +0.30 m (+18 %; the estimate was too long, consistent with the two triangulation runs below). One run, one door, one day.
Observed weakness: 36 of the 51 steps were turn pulses hunting around the 4 deg window (+8 / -8 deg alternation: the shortest useful pulse turns more than 4 deg). Planned: accept about 8 deg before driving, or a shorter pulse. The "pulse and re-check" scheme itself (never trust the open-loop heading, re-measure after every move) is what made the arrival work and is the candidate strategy for the approach.

### Why not just a better model? (research and offline tests on the same 793 frames, 2026-11-11 evening)
- **Regime, not model:** faces of 22-40 px at the sensor are in the TinyFace regime (6-32 px high, average about 20). Models that exceed 99 % on LFW score only about 50-60 % Rank-1 there (published figures: about 59 % cited in a review, 46-50 % for recent models; super-resolution pipelines reach about 47 % Rank-1, FaceMoE gains about 2.3 points). Close-range face unlock (a laptop or phone at 0.3-0.5 m, 150-300 px faces, frontal, often infrared or depth) is a different regime. Fine-tuning on TinyFace itself is reported to hurt the other resolutions.
- **Offline tests (descriptive; 43 entries, leave-one-member-out, `scripts/d2_entry_level.py`, `scripts/d2_model_compare.py`, ROC AUC where 0.5 = no separation):** frame level buffalo_s 0.710; entry level (mean embedding per entry) 0.735; face width at least 30 px only: 0.63 (frame) / 0.68 (entry), a smaller sample; upsampling the frame x2 before detection and embedding: 0.702 / 0.728 (no gain). At the 0.45 threshold the entry-level mean gives TAR 0.64 at FAR 0.28 (frame level 0.34 at 0.026 with this protocol; the pilot tool reports 0.399 at 0.066): the mean embedding raises both, it does not separate better. **buffalo_l (ResNet50, `w600k_r50`) on the same frames (`scripts/d2_model_compare.py`, `data/d2_model_compare_1111.json`): frame level AUC 0.787, entry level 0.843** (buffalo_s 0.710 / 0.735; x2 upsampling: 0.781 / 0.838): a larger recogniser is worth +0.08 to +0.11 AUC, the largest gain of anything tried tonight, but 0.84 is still far from a usable verifier at 22-53 px (792 of 793 frames embedded, 42 impostor and 39 distinct genuine entries (78 scores, each entry counted in two folds)). Operating points: buffalo_l frame level TAR 0.50 at FAR 5 % and **0.32 at FAR 1 %**, entry level 0.67 and 0.41 at the thresholds that were labelled 5 % and 1 %, but with only 42 impostor entries the realised FAR is 3/42 = 7.1 % and 1/42 = 2.4 % (the script interpolated the quantile; audit 2026-11-12); frame level is exact (40/793 = 5.0 %, 8/793 = 1.0 %); buffalo_s frame level 0.40 / 0.23, entry 0.38 / 0.38. The impostor 99th-percentile score is about 0.53 (buffalo_l) and 0.50 (buffalo_s) at frame level, i.e. above the deployed 0.45 threshold: at 0.45 these faces give FAR well above 1 %. buffalo_l is a stock InsightFace pack (a 282 MB download that stalled twice before finishing), not yet in the deployed pipeline (buffalo_s, chosen for speed): a candidate for HNHL's perception evaluation together with the resolution fix.
- **Training on our three identities would not be a valid fix:** the whitelist is arbitrary registered occupants, three identities overfit, and the same people are the test. A legitimate training route is a low-resolution recipe on a large public set (resolution augmentation, quality-adaptive margin such as AdaFace) and a lot of compute on an RTX 4050 6 GB for an expected gain of a few points, against a 2.5x pixel gain from the sensor.
- **The cheaper lever is pixels:** the door ESP32 runs `esp32/sentry_cam` at `FRAMESIZE_VGA` (640x480); an OV2640 does hardware JPEG up to UXGA (1600x1200), i.e. up to 2.5x the face width at the same place (22-40 px to 55-100 px, above the pilot's 45 px floor). Cost: frame rate over the 2.4 GHz link (20 MHz clock gave 1.1 fps, 10 MHz gives 9-10 fps at VGA; larger frames are bandwidth-bound), a reflash or the web-UI control (port 80 did not answer tonight). Measured camera-to-door distance 2.11 m (inside 1.5-3.0 m): the face width of 30 px at 2.11 m means a lens of about 77 degrees; a closer or less tilted placement and a narrower lens add pixels too.

### ESP32-CAM performance research (2026-11-11 evening, for the dorm test; web sources, not our measurements)
- **Frame rate falls steeply with frame size.** The only systematic benchmark found (Nowroz et al., arXiv 2505.24081, one AI-Thinker ESP32-CAM, OV2640, JPEG over HTTP/Wi-Fi, 15 min per size; XCLK, JPEG quality, PSRAM, Wi-Fi band and client are NOT stated, so it is a reference, not a prediction): QVGA 44.4 fps, VGA 14.2, SVGA 8.3, XGA 3.4, SXGA 2.3, UXGA 1.3 (file size 15.6 KB at VGA, 67.6 KB at SXGA, 108 KB at UXGA); MCU temperature 70-74 C at every size, no throughput effect; input voltage below about 2.985 V hurts, so a regulated supply matters. Our board at VGA gave 9.1-9.9 fps (firmware note: XCLK 20 MHz gave 1.1 fps through interference, 10 MHz is used), below the paper's 14.2: the link, not the sensor, limits it. Expect about 2 fps at SXGA and about 1.3 fps at UXGA on this link: one walk-in of 3-5 s still gives 5-10 frames.
- **Pixels per face:** SXGA (1280x1024) doubles the VGA face width (22-40 px to about 44-80 px), UXGA 2.5x (about 55-100 px), XGA 1.6x. Community rule of thumb: under 60-80 px poor recognition (not a benchmark).
- **Cheap settings, unmeasured gains:** Wi-Fi sleep off (`WiFi.setSleep(false)`), latest-frame grab and two buffers with PSRAM (our firmware already uses `CAMERA_GRAB_LATEST`, `fb_count` 2), a stable 5 V supply (the tower should use a power bank or adapter, not the laptop port). MJPEG streams serve one client efficiently (known: close every other reader).
- **Optics:** a swapped M12 lens (Instructables "ESP32-Cam M12 Lens Hack"; 6-8 mm lenses sold in sets) narrows the field of view and enlarges faces at the same resolution. Unverified here: the field of view of an M12 lens depends on the sensor size (a printed 69.5 degrees refers to a larger sensor, on the OV2640 it would be narrower), the back-focus of longer lenses and the thread pitch (M12 x 0.5) must match. Not tested for face range by any source found.
- **Other boards (no measured streaming comparison found):** XIAO ESP32S3 Sense (8 MB PSRAM, external antenna, sensor swappable to OV5640, 5 MP), ESP32-S3 camera boards; OV5640 reports of overheating. A new board means new firmware and the spec names OV2640/GC2145.
- **Related projects use the same architecture as ours** (ESP32-CAM MJPEG -> Python on a PC -> face_recognition/dlib or similar, e.g. the DEV Community weekend project, `jjateen/esp32-cam-security`, `h-enes-simsek/securityCam` with a Flask relay for several viewers); none used InsightFace, and on-device ESP32 face recognition is described as unsuitable for identification. Our pipeline (InsightFace on the laptop) is the stronger variant; the open problem is the pixels at the sensor.
Sources: arXiv 2505.24081; s60sc/ESP32-CAM_MJPEG2SD (UXGA at most about 10 fps when recording to SD, 20 MHz clock); Espressif/Arduino camera web server examples; Seeed XIAO ESP32S3 Sense documentation; Instructables ESP32-Cam M12 Lens Hack; JLCPCB ESP32-CAM guide.

### Code audit, continued by hand with Serena (2026-11-12 evening; function-level reads, every query restricted to a file)
- **`jetson/robot_udp.py` (MEDIUM, safety, found by reading `main()` and the systemd unit): no SIGTERM handler.** Python's default SIGTERM action terminates the process without running the `finally` that zeroes the motors, and the motor driver keeps its last PWM. `sentry-control.service` stops the service with `pkill -f jetson/robot_udp.py` (SIGTERM) on every stop and restart (`Restart=always`, `RestartSec=5`), so a restart or kill while moving with R1 held left the wheels at the last command for at least 5 s (the e-stop on an R1 release still cuts them). **Fix:** a SIGTERM handler that raises `SystemExit` so the `finally` runs, and the final stop retries once. The file's docstring claimed a stop "on exit, on any exception", which did not cover SIGTERM. Windows cannot deliver SIGTERM to a handler, so the unit tests (`tests/test_controller.py`, 21 passed) cannot show it; **it was tested on the robot on 2026-11-13** (next bullet: the log shows `motors +0.00 +0.00` with packets still arriving, then the service restarted).
- **SIGTERM fix tested on the robot (2026-11-13 evening, dorm, wheels up, e-stop armed, R1 held, 12.35 V):** the robot's `robot_udp.py` was the old version (no handler) and its `common/mixing.py` too old for the new file (no `apply_trim`): the first restart crashed with an ImportError, so **the control service was down for about a minute until `common/mixing.py` was updated** (no trim is set in `calib/motion.json` on either side, so the driving is unchanged). After the update, a v = 0.5 command stream (125 packets accepted, motors +0.15) was cut by `sudo docker exec jetbot_jupyter pkill -TERM -f jetson/robot_udp.py` (the way systemd stops the unit): the log shows `motors +0.00 +0.00` while packets were still arriving, then the service restarted by itself (new pid). The first attempt did not test it: `pkill` through `docker exec` needs sudo for the `jetbot` user. Backups on the robot: `robot_udp.py.bak_1113`, `mixing.py.bak_1113`. The robot's other deployed files were compared line by line with the repo (Windows against Unix line endings ignored): `edge_bench.py`, `espcam.py`, `face_post.py`, `mjpeg_probe.py`, `parity_run.py`, `trt_runtime.py` are identical; **`jetson/mjpeg_latest.py` on the robot is the older version** (no bounded buffer or stale-frame rejection, blocking `read()`), which matters for any bench that reads the ESP32 stream (`--door-url`). Not measured: the physical stop time of the wheels.
- Checked and found sound: `Controller.on_packet` / `on_tick` (e-stop ignores motor commands, a stop is repeated every 0.25 s while the latch is on, the watchdog stops once after 0.3 s of silence), `estop_engaged` (fails safe on a missing, unreadable, stale or future heartbeat; monotonic stamp), `common/mixing.py` (`mix` rescales both wheels, non-finite input gives zero, `check_drive` validates the parameters, `deadzone` zeroes a wheel below `min_pwm`: a mixed command can therefore stop one wheel and pivot instead of arcing, which the tools avoid by sending legal commands).
- **`sentry/net/udp_tx.py` (MEDIUM, safety, verified by a script): a stop sent with `send(0.0, 0.0)` could be silently dropped.** `UDPSender.send` skips a call made less than one period (33 ms) after the previous one and returns `False`; callers ignore the return value. Verified: `send(0.5, 0)` then `send(0, 0)` 20 ms later returned `False` and only `stop()` put a zero on the wire. 12 call sites in 7 tools (`find_door`, `approach_door`, `scan_boards`, `motion_trial`, `motion_check`, `follow_marker`, `dr_path`) stop this way. The robot's 300 ms watchdog bounds the effect (up to about 10 cm of extra coasting at 0.34 m/s), but a stop should not depend on it. **Fix:** a zero command is never dropped by the limiter (only a repeated zero inside one period is skipped); regression test `test_a_stop_is_never_dropped_by_the_rate_limiter` (fails on the old code, passes on the new; 703 tests pass).
- `jetson/estop.py` read in full (`EstopLogic`, `main`, `stop`, `go`, the I2C helpers): sound; every failure direction ends stopped (startup, any other button, a lost pad, an unknown pad state at connection, a stale heartbeat), the motor cut precedes the latch write, a failed cut is logged and `robot_udp` still zeroes through its own driver. **Suggestion (LOW, needs a measurement on the robot first):** a dead e-stop process stops the robot only when its heartbeat is older than 2.0 s (`estop_engaged`), so up to 2 s (about 0.7 m at 0.35 m/s) of motion; the heartbeat is written every 0.5 s, so a 1.0 s limit would halve that if the loop jitter allows. Also noted: the arm chord completes with R1 already held (ARM then GO at once).

- **Live tools' cleanup paths (read by grep and function):** `pursue_live`, `deploy_live`, `explore_live` and `link_cut_test` stop their sender in a `finally` through `close(stop=True)` (which bypasses the rate limiter), set `done` on any exit and join with a timeout (`explore_live` joins without one: LOW, a hung SSH watcher can stop the process from exiting, the stop has already been sent). Sound.
- **ESP32 firmware diff (`DQK-claude/esp32-opt`, read in `app_httpd.cpp`):** no critical issue. **LOW:** (1) `stats_handler` passes the `snprintf` return value to `httpd_resp_send`; if the JSON ever exceeded the 600-byte buffer (today about 420) it would read past the buffer: clamp `n` to `sizeof(b) - 1`; (2) `stream_set_sndbuf` blocks the HTTP task on `xSemaphoreTake(..., portMAX_DELAY)` for the lwIP callback: a lost callback would hang the stream (a timeout needs the request struct on the heap, not the stack); (3) the diagnostics counters are `volatile` but not atomic (a 64-bit read on the 32-bit chip can tear: cosmetic); (4) the send-buffer patch relies on lwIP internals of core 2.0.17 (`lwip_socket_dbg_get_socket`, the first member of `lwip_sock`): rebuild-time risk if the core is upgraded; (5) the debug endpoints (`/reg`, `/xclk`, `/pll`, `/resolution`) are unauthenticated and compiled in. The evade pulse is bounded by `evade_duration_s` (0.4 s) in `tick`. Not exercised on the robot.
- **`sentry/app/target_queue.py` (LOW, replay only): the queue's active record is never completed or dropped by production code.** `Phase2Pipeline` calls `pop()` only on STANDBY to PURSUE, and `complete_active` / `drop_active` are called only in tests, so in a replay with two intruders the second `pop` would overwrite `_active_target` and leave the first as status "active" in no list. `Phase2Pipeline` is used only by `tools/replay_phase2.py`; the live path uses the state machine's own queue, so this does not affect the robot. Not fixed (needs a decision on which queue is the source of truth).
- **`sentry/net/` (`zmq_rx`, `clock_sync`, `discover`, read in full): no defect found.** `ZMQReceiver.read(timeout)` returns None on timeout, consumes the frame (so a frame is never returned twice), raises if the worker died, and close joins the thread; the age of a frame is the caller's check on `t_rx` (the live tools do it). `synchronize` ignores late or wrong echoes and raises instead of inventing an offset. LOW: `find_esp32` takes the first host with port 81 open, so on a network with another such host it could pick the wrong one (give the IP explicitly); `find` assumes a /24.
- **`sentry/nav/controllers.py` (MEDIUM, behaviour, verified by a script; not fixed, needs a robot test): the pursuit obstacle deflection is clamped to almost nothing.** In `PursuitController.compute`, branch 5 (translating forward) limits |w| to `v - dead_zone - 0.01` so that the slower wheel stays above the dead zone. With the dead zone 0.12/0.3 = 0.40 and v at least 0.45, that limit is 0.04 to 0.14, so the configured `obstacle_avoid_w` = 0.45 never takes effect while driving: target 2.5 m ahead, obstacle dead ahead at 0.45 m or 0.40 m gives (v, w) = (0.55, -0.14), not a 0.45 deflection. Forward speed is cut only below `obstacle_critical_m` (0.35 m), where the robot stops and spins away. So between 0.5 m and 0.35 m the robot keeps driving at 0.55 with a weak turn, and the real avoidance is the stop at 0.35 m. Before any pursuit run near furniture: either slow down inside the 0.5 m band (cap v at 0.45 and let the wheel mix keep the dead-zone rule) or spin in place at the threshold, and measure the stopping distance with wheels on the floor. A test pins the current behaviour only for the critical case.
  **Added after reading `tools/pursue_live.py`: the live pursuit tool does not use this obstacle code at all.** Its detector is person-only (class 0), no obstacle distance is passed to `PursuitController`, and the tool itself prints "NO OBSTACLE AVOIDANCE". So the clamp cannot be tested with `pursue_live` on the robot; it only matters once obstacle input is wired into pursuit. Only `tools/explore_live.py` (the exploration controller, 0.6 m repulsion, furniture classes only: chair, couch, plant, bed, table, tv, fridge, suitcase) avoids obstacles on the robot, and a cardboard box is not one of those classes.
- **`jetson/mjpeg_latest.py` (LOW, found in the 13/11 bench):** `reconnects` is incremented once more when the receiver stops (the loop exit is counted), so every bench reports at least 1 reconnect (I first read it as a stream drop; the ESP32 frame rate 12.46 / 12.54 fps shows there was none).
